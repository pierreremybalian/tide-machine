# Decisions

Non-obvious choices, deviations from HANDOFF.md, and why.

## Stack
- **Inherited from Block Universe** (same lockfile, same pinned versions): render core, scroll driver, caption schedule, narration with status chips and dual clock, HUD card system, labels, panels, loupe, rig, build scripts. Dev port 4360.

## Tide prediction (validated against NOAA's official hourly predictions, January and July 2026, 25 ports)
- **Astronomy**: Meeus polynomials for s, h, p, N, p′ and obliquity; Schureman (SP-98) for I, ξ, ν, ν′, ν″, P; τ = T + h − s with T from the UT Julian day (ΔT ignored: < 0.001° in s).
- **Constituent conventions that differ from a literal reading of Schureman**, each chosen because it matches NOAA:
  - **S1**: V = T (not T + 180°). With +180° the diurnal residual at Philadelphia was 3.6 cm (twice S1's amplitude); with V = T, 0.2 cm.
  - **M1**: NOAA's M1 (14.4966939°/h) is the compound N2 − O1 (NO1) for u and f. Schureman's M1 node factors left a 1–2 cm diurnal residual; the compound brings the mean RMS from 1.02 to 0.72 cm.
  - **L2**: u = u(M2), f = Schureman's f(L2) (dropping his R term). Large-tide ports improve by half in January (Eastport 3.0 → 1.55 cm) at a small cost in July.
- **Result**: San Francisco 0.82 cm RMS (max 2.3), The Battery 0.39 cm (max 1.3); 21 of 24 other ports < 1 cm; worst Seattle 1.75 cm (Puget Sound's shallow-water constituents beyond the 37) and Eastport 1.55 cm (M2 is 2.6 m there).
- **Anchorage** replaced by **Juneau**: NOAA predicts Anchorage with 120 constituents; with the standard 37 our error was 23 cm RMS.
- **Harmonic analysis** fits one year of SF hourly data (Sa and Ssa excluded: a single year cannot separate them from weather) and recovers NOAA's M2, K1, O1, S2, N2 within 1.5 cm and 3°.

## Finale data (EOT20 tiles, NOAA client, places)
- **Sources and licences.** EOT20 (Hart-Davis, Piccioni, Dettmering, Schwatke, Passaro, Seitz 2021, DGFI-TUM; SEANOE https://doi.org/10.17882/79489; paper https://doi.org/10.5194/essd-13-3869-2021), **CC BY 4.0**: the credit and our changes are written into `public/data/eot20/index.json` and must appear in the page's sources. EOT20 is altimetry between 66°S and 66°N and FES2014b poleward (SEANOE distributes the whole product as CC BY 4.0). NOAA CO-OPS station list, constants, datums and water levels: US government, public domain. Places: Natural Earth 10m populated places and coastline v5.1.2, public domain.
- **EOT20 conventions, checked in the files and against NOAA.** netCDF amplitude in cm, phase in degrees; `real + i·imag = A·e^{iφ}` exactly; land is the fill value 0. The phase is a Greenwich phase lag, the same κ as NOAA and `src/tides/predict.ts`: at open-coast gauges EOT20's nearest node matches NOAA to ≤ 1.6 cm and ≤ 3.3° for M2 and ≤ 1 cm and ≤ 2° for K1 (Santa Monica, Santa Barbara, Monterey, Crescent City, Atlantic City, Bermuda, Kwajalein, Wake, Guam). Gauges inside harbours and straits lag the open water in the semidiurnal band: M2 at Honolulu −13.8°, San Francisco −13.6°, The Battery −26.7° (amplitudes 0.1, 1.4, 2.1 cm off); K1 0.8°, 3.4°, 9.7°.
- **Tiles: 10°, not HANDOFF's 5°** (half the requests and files for the same bytes). 561 tiles named by their south-west corner (`n30w130.bin`), 81×81 nodes at the native 1/8° including both edges (so bilinear never needs a neighbour), nine constituents (M2 S2 N2 K2 K1 O1 P1 Q1 M4) as int16 mm + uint16 0.01°, stored nodes only behind a bit mask; all-land tiles skipped. Layout: `src/finale/eot20.ts`.
- **Size: 41 MB** (tiles 38.3 MB, overview 2.8 MB). The full 1/8° ocean would be 99 MB, so ocean more than 1° (8 nodes) from land is kept only on the 1/4° lattice, except where that would change any constant by more than 0.5 cm (Great Barrier Reef lagoon, Aleutian passes, the EOT20/FES2014b seam at 66°N), which keeps 36.7% of ocean nodes; the thinned nodes' 1/4° error is 0.03 cm RMS, 0.5 cm max. The sampler interpolates on the 1/4° lattice only when a missing corner is off-lattice and the surrounding 1/4° cell is complete; otherwise missing corners are land.
- **`overview.bin`**: the whole globe at 1/2° (M2, S2, K1, O1), for the finale globe's tint and stage 3's co-tidal map, which cannot load 561 tiles.
- **Sampling.** Complex bilinear interpolation of H·e^{iκ} (never phase directly); land corners dropped and weights renormalised; on land, the nearest ocean node within 30 km; beyond that the point is "inland" (`TideSourceError`).
- **Tide source.** NOAA harmonic station within 25 km → measured (constants + datums from the NOAA API at request time; z0 = MSL − MLLW, or relative to MSL when NOAA lists no MLLW). Else EOT20 → modelled, z0 = 0 relative to mean sea level, stated in the note. If NOAA is unreachable the model stands in and the note says so. The 1,374-station list ships as `public/data/noaa-harcon-stations.json` (80 KB, with a `live` flag for the 238 that are also real-time water-level gauges), so the nearest-station lookup needs no request. Live water level: `product=water_level&range=24`, MLLW, retried in MSL, `null` on any failure; `toDatum()` converts between them.
- **Modelled accuracy** (EOT20's nine constituents + NOAA's z0, against NOAA's official hourly predictions; RMS about the monthly mean, January / July 2026): Honolulu 3.4 / 3.4 cm, Atlantic City 4.1 / 4.3, San Diego 5.9 / 5.9, Newport OR 9.2 / 9.9, Key West 8.9 / 8.3. NOAA's own constants for the same nine give 1.6, 3.4–3.7, 3.8–4.0, 5.6–6.0, 2.1–2.4 cm: on open coasts most of the error is the 28 minor constituents EOT20 lacks, in harbours it is the model. Monthly mean offsets of 2–15 cm come on top: EOT20 has no Sa/Ssa seasonal cycle and no datum. State it as "several centimetres on open coasts, more in bays and estuaries".
- **Places**: 300 coastal places in 158 countries for the search box (`public/data/places.json`, 12 KB), from Natural Earth populated places within 20 km of the Natural Earth coastline and within 30 km of an EOT20 ocean node: every coastal country's largest place, every coastal capital, a few famous tides (Saint John, Broome, Incheon, Bhavnagar, Río Gallegos …), then by population with at most 8 per country (16 for the US). Typed coordinates are accepted too. `node scripts/fetch-places.ts` rebuilds this and the NOAA list.
- **Rebuild**: `python3 -m venv scripts/.venv && scripts/.venv/bin/pip install -r scripts/requirements.txt`, then `scripts/.venv/bin/python -I scripts/eot20_tiles.py` (downloads the 2.3 GB zip once into the gitignored `scripts/cache/`), then `node scripts/fetch-places.ts`.
- **CSP**: the browser calls `https://api.tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov` (connect-src); tiles and lists are same-origin.

## Machine (src/tides/machine.ts, src/shared/brass, src/scenes/_machine)
- **Sources.** Kelvin: W. Thomson, "The tide gauge, tidal harmonic analyser, and tide predicter", *Minutes of Proceedings of the ICE* vol. 65 (1881), pp. 2–25 and discussion (https://archive.org/details/minutesproceedi00secrgoog); Thomson & Tait, *Treatise on Natural Philosophy* (1879), Part I, Appendix B (https://archive.org/details/treatiseonnatur01darwgoog); Science Museum Group object 1876-1129 (https://collection.sciencemuseumgroup.org.uk/objects/co53901/william-thomsons-tide-predicting-machine-1872). Old Brass Brains: US Coast and Geodetic Survey **Special Publication No. 32** (1915), *Description of the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey Tide-Predicting Machine No. 2* (https://archive.org/details/descriptionofusc00uscouoft); NOAA, "History of tide predictions" (https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/predhist.html) and "Tide Predicting Machine No. 2" (https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/predmach.html); T. Ehret, "Old Brass Brains", *ACSM Bulletin* 233 (2008) (https://web.archive.org/web/20110728172441/http://www.webmazine.org/issues/bull233/documents/oldBrassBrains.pdf); NOAA brochure "Old Brass Brains" (1989).
- **Kelvin's ten**: M2, S2, N2, K1, O1, K2, L2, P1, M4, MS4, in Thomson's order from the pen end (ICE vol. 65 p. 18; Thomson & Tait App. B). SP-32 (p. 10, Table B) lists Q1 instead of O1; Thomson's own text wins. Designed 1872 (Tower's chain-and-pulley idea, August 1872), built by A. Légé & Co., shown at the British Association's Bradford meeting in September 1873; Science Museum: 1820 × 1000 × 770 mm, 86 kg. "A year's tides for any port in about four hours." Two rows of shafts, neighbouring shafts one pulley diameter apart so the free wire runs vertically; a fine wire, one end fixed, the other hanging with the pen (an ink bottle as the weight), passing alternately over and under the pulleys; the paper moved by a vertical cylinder geared to the shafts.
- **Kelvin's linkage**: his first machine carried each pulley on the crank pin itself (the pulley swings sideways as well as up and down; true harmonic slides came with the second, India Office machine). We draw Scotch yokes for both machines (as the brief asks and Old Brass Brains had); the model's `linkage: 'crank'` quantifies Kelvin's original: at our proportions it bends San Francisco's week by 3.0 cm RMS (6.2 cm max), the error Thomson accepted while the total range stayed under about 30 cm.
- **Kelvin's gearing**: no tooth counts survive (Roberts computed them; Légé's layout used worms). Ours are the closest ratios realisable with two spur stages of 10–200 teeth from a shaft turning once per mean solar day (15°/h, so S2 is exactly 2:1), found by exhaustive search; drift per Julian year: M2 −0.019°, S2 0, N2 +0.007°, K1 +0.020°, O1 +0.006°, K2 +0.039°, L2 −0.008°, P1 −0.008°, M4 −0.044°, MS4 −0.022°. Labelled "approximation" in the model.
- **Kelvin's wire direction** is not documented; ours meets the first pulley over the top, which leaves M2 (the biggest term) in the lower row beside the pen, consistent with the rows spreading apart towards the pen end in the photographs (Thomson's "<"). We reproduce that spread: each pulley's rest height clears its neighbours' throws.
- **Old Brass Brains**: R. A. Harris (design) and E. G. Fischer (construction; Ehret spells it Fisher). Design accepted 1895, construction from 1896, complete February 1910, first used for the 1912 tables; NOAA: machine predictions 1912–1965, computers from 1966. 129¼ in long, 74¼ in high, 24 in wide (SP-32 p. 15); about 2,500 lb (NOAA, Ehret; SP-32 gives no weight). Steel box-chronometer chain over pulleys 2 in in diameter on shafts 2 in apart, cosine (height) and sine (time) sides; dials for the times and heights of high and low water, and a curve on paper 6 in wide fed 1 ft per day. Setting up a station 2–3 h, running and copying a year 7–12 h. Classified during the Second World War and used for the Normandy and Pacific landings (Ehret 2008). On display at NOAA, Silver Spring.
- **Old Brass Brains' layout**: SP-32 lists each range (row) of the rear frame front to rear and says the chain runs "alternately downward under one pulley and upward over the next" from its fixed end; the front frame held M2, S2 and K1 on two shafts each at half amplitude (40 height pulleys). We give each of the 37 components one pulley and keep SP-32's order, chain from the rear (Sa) to the front (M2), rows alternating from "under" at the fixed end (`OBB_37`).
- **Old Brass Brains' gearing**: SP-32 Table C tooth counts I/II × III/IV from vertical driving shafts at 30° per dial hour (worms on Mm, Mf, MSf, Sa, Ssa; Sa has a further 60:120). All 37 rows recomputed and matching SP-32's gear speeds (test). Drift per year −0.75° (ρ1) to +0.48° (K2); SP-32 quotes errors as true − gear per 8,760 h, we report realised − true per Julian year. **M1** is the exception: the machine's M1 (14.4920521°/h) is Schureman's, not NOAA's present M1 (14.4966939°/h, the N2 − O1 compound our constants use), so that crank gets the best approximation instead.
- **The wire identity, honestly.** The wire's length is laid by tangent geometry (straight runs between tangent points, arcs on each pulley), not by the formula. With vertical runs a pulley that moves δ moves the free end 2δ: the pen moves 2 Σ throw·cos θ, and the reading divides by 2 · scale. Tests: with exact speeds and node factors reset continuously the pen equals `predict()` for San Francisco to < 1e-13 m over a week; with the gear trains and settings frozen at the start of the week (as on the machine) the pen stays within 0.56 mm (Kelvin) and 0.48 mm (OBB) of `predict()`, almost all of it from node corrections u frozen at the start, not from the gears. **Kelvin's 10 vs the 37** at San Francisco, a year hourly: 6.5 cm RMS, 20 cm max (Sa, Ssa, Q1, NU2, J1 … are missing).
- **Schematic, stated**: the gear trains are drawn with real tooth counts, but their arrangement is ours: each crank's train rises (or falls) from a horizontal driving shaft per row through a mitre pair, with an idler where one stage would otherwise be too big; Old Brass Brains used vertical driving shafts with bevel gears. Worm stages are drawn as threaded rods at the wheel's rim. The record is a flat paper band fed from a supply drum past the pen onto a take-up roll, showing the last 2.5 days; the wire runs sideways over two fixed guides to reach it (fixed guides change nothing in the sum). Crank throw is 0.4 machine units per metre of tide (machine units ≈ 5 in, pulleys 2 in across as on Old Brass Brains).
- **Rendering**: the outline is a TSL port of Watch Academy's G-buffer edge pass using three r186 `pass()` with `mrt()` (fill colour, view normal, ident = part id, line gain, highlight, fill) and a composite that runs the Roberts cross on linear depth plus normal creases (30°) and part-id changes; part ids are a vertex attribute so static parts merge into one mesh per style. Luminous overlays (wire, crank pins, paper trace) render additively after the composite with depth test off; they `Discard` fragments that add nothing because on WebGL2 (ANGLE/Metal in Chromium) zero-valued additive fragments were drawn black. Squares are written as products (`pow` of a negative base is NaN) and quantities that must be interpolated per pixel go through `vertexStage` (a corner squared per vertex is 2 everywhere).
- **Cost** (headless Chromium, WebGPU, this Mac, uncapped): Kelvin 88 solid draw calls, 0.20 M triangles, ~0.7 ms per frame; Old Brass Brains 298 draw calls, 0.63 M triangles, ~0.9 ms; `update()` 7 µs and 25 µs, no allocation. Gear tooth detail follows the quality tier.
