A tide prediction answers one question: where would the water stand if only the Moon and the Sun were acting on it? Everything else the sea does is left out. Most days the leftovers are small. Wind pushes water toward a coast or away from it, low air pressure lets the sea bulge up beneath it (about a centimeter for each hectopascal), and rivers in flood add their share. At the Battery these effects usually amount to a few tens of centimeters.

Hurricane Sandy turned west into New Jersey on the evening of October 29, 2012. Its wind field was enormous, and for two days it drove water from the open Atlantic into the New York Bight, a corner of coastline where the shore turns ninety degrees and water has nowhere else to go. At the Battery the surge, measured minus predicted, grew from about 20 centimeters on the morning of the 27th to almost three meters at 9:24 p.m. on the 29th.

Two pieces of bad luck stacked up. The surge peaked within an hour of the evening’s high tide, and the Moon was full that day, so the tide itself was a little higher than average. Had the same surge arrived at low tide, the peak would have been about 1.3 meters lower.

The map shows Lower Manhattan, the Brooklyn and New Jersey waterfronts and the rivers between them. The gold line follows the shore at the predicted level; the water beyond it is the surge. Where the real flood went depended on seawalls, drains and tunnels the terrain model does not know about, so read the map as the shape of the problem, not a flood survey.
