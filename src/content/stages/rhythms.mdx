Newton’s two bulges are the ideal case: the Moon over the equator, at a fixed distance, alone. The real Moon is none of these. Its orbit is tilted, so the bulges tilt with it and the two tides of a day differ. It is an ellipse, so the pull strengthens and weakens every 27.6 days. The Sun adds its own versions of each. Written out as sums of steady waves, these effects become extra rhythms beside M2: N2 and L2 for the ellipse, K1, O1 and P1 for the tilt, K2 for the tilted pair together, and dozens more, smaller.

Each rhythm’s speed is fixed by astronomy, and is the same in every port on Earth. What differs from port to port is how the ocean responds: how high each rhythm stands there and how late it arrives. San Francisco answers the once-a-day rhythms strongly, which is why its tide is “mixed”, with a higher and a lower high water, and a higher and a lower low water, most days.

Some lines are not astronomical at all. The yearly Sa and half-yearly Ssa are the seasons, from warming, winds and river flow. The overtides M4, MS4, MK3 and their relatives are the tide distorting itself in shallow water. Both are fitted the same way, because they repeat at known speeds, and a predictor that ignored them would be wrong by centimeters every day.
