The Sun is 27 million times more massive than the Moon and 390 times farther away. The tide-raising effect goes as mass divided by the cube of distance, so the Sun’s bulges come out at 0.46 of the Moon’s: 16 cm against 36 cm in the equilibrium picture. They are aligned with the Sun, so they come round once per solar day, every 12 hours, while the Moon’s come round every 12 hours 25 minutes.

Two rhythms with nearly the same period beat against each other. The dial shows it as two arms: M2 turning at 28.984° an hour, S2 at exactly 30°. The arm lengths are San Francisco’s measured amplitudes and their angles are set from NOAA’s phases for the date on screen. Seen from M2’s own arm, S2 creeps around once every 14.77 days, half the 29.53-day cycle of the Moon’s phases. When the arms line up the tides are big; when they oppose, the tides are small.

Only M2 and S2 are drawn here, so the envelope is a clean wave. San Francisco’s real record is messier: the once-a-day rhythms from the Moon’s tilt beat with their own period, close to 13.66 days, and the Moon’s distance swells and shrinks M2 over 27.6 days. The next stage lines all of them up.
