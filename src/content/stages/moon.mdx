Gravity falls off with the square of distance. Across Earth’s 12,700 km the Moon’s pull changes by about 7 percent, from the side facing it to the side facing away. What matters for the ocean is that difference, not the pull itself: Earth and everything on it fall toward the Moon together, so a uniform pull would leave the water where it is. Subtract the pull on Earth’s center from the pull at each place and what remains points outward along the Earth–Moon line on both sides and inward around the middle. That is why there are two bulges, not one.

The bulge heights on screen are the equilibrium tide, the shape an ocean would take if it settled instantly into balance with that leftover pull: 36 cm at the points under and opposite the Moon, 18 cm below the mean in a ring between them. The scene draws them a million times higher and puts the Moon ten times closer than it is (60 Earth radii), and over the equator, where in fact it swings up to 28° north and south every month. Those simplifications return as rhythms of their own in stage 6.

Earth turns once in 23 h 56 min against the stars, but the Moon moves along its orbit in the same direction, so a place has to turn a little further to come back under it. The interval between two passes under the Moon is the lunar day, 24 h 50 min, and the twice-daily tide repeats every half of it: 12 h 25 min. The equilibrium picture gets that rhythm exactly right. It gets the size and timing wrong, because a real ocean is a wave in basins bounded by continents, and it cannot keep up with a bulge sweeping around the planet at 1,600 km an hour.
