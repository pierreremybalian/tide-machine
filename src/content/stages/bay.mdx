Waves change as the water shallows because their speed depends on depth. A wave of a given period, here 11 seconds between crests, keeps that period all the way to the beach. In deep water it travels at 17 meters a second and its crests are 189 meters apart. Once the depth is less than about half that spacing, the bottom holds it back: at 20 meters it moves at 12 m/s, at 10 meters 9.4 m/s, at 5 meters 6.8 m/s. The crests behind catch up with the crests in front, so they bunch together and the same energy piles into a taller, steeper wave. Where one part of a crest is over shallower water than another, that part slows first and the crest swings round, which is why waves arrive nearly parallel to a beach whatever direction they came from. When a wave stands about 0.8 times as high as the water is deep, it breaks.

The tide is the same kind of wave taken to an extreme. Its crests are hundreds of kilometers apart, far more than any depth on Earth, so it always feels the bottom and always travels at √(g·h): about 200 meters a second in the open ocean, 10 meters a second where the bay is 10 meters deep, slower still over the mud. That is why high water arrives at Redwood City an hour after the Golden Gate, and why the delta end of the bay, reached through the narrow Carquinez Strait, lags by more than two hours.

The South Bay amplifies the tide because it is closed at the far end. The incoming wave reflects and returns, and the two add: high water is higher and low water lower than at the Gate. The range at Coyote Creek is a third larger than at the Golden Gate. The northern bays work the other way: friction in the long, narrow straits drains energy, so Suisun Bay’s range is about two-thirds of the Gate’s.

On screen, the color of the water is its height above local mean sea level, interpolated between 35 NOAA stations. Watch the bright band of high water move from the ocean into the bay and on into the South Bay while the Gate is already falling. That is a wave inside a basin, not a level rising everywhere at once.
