Kelvin’s idea spread quickly. William Ferrel designed the first American machine for the Coast and Geodetic Survey, and it made the US tables from 1885 to 1911. Its successor, designed by Rollin Harris and built in the Survey’s own office by E. G. Fischer, took from 1896 to 1910 to complete. It summed 37 components, including five long-period ones, and was the first machine to give the times and heights of high and low water directly on dials, as well as drawing the curve.

It came to be known as Old Brass Brains. Setting it for a port meant choosing the throw of each crank from that port’s harmonic constants and turning each one to its starting angle for the first day of the year. Then an operator ran it through the year and copied the time and height of each high and low water from its dials into the tables.

Without a machine, NOAA notes, the harmonic method “would involve too much labor to be of practical service.” Old Brass Brains made the official predictions until 1965; from 1966 electronic computers did the same sum. The machine is on display at NOAA in Silver Spring, Maryland.
