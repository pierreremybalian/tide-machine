By the 1860s Thomson had shown that a port’s tide could be written as a sum of simple waves, each with a speed fixed by astronomy and a height and timing measured at that port. Predicting a year meant adding a dozen sine waves for every hour of it, by hand. In 1872, following a suggestion from the engineer Beauchamp Tower to use a chain over pulleys, he designed a machine to do the adding.

Each wave gets a crank. The crank turns at the wave’s speed through gears from a shaft the operator turns by hand, its pin is clamped at a distance from the center proportional to the wave’s height, and its starting angle is set from the wave’s timing at that port and the position of the Moon and Sun on the first day. The pin moves a pulley up and down. A single wire runs under and over all the pulleys, so its free end, with a pen on it, rises and falls by the sum of their motions and draws the tide on a moving sheet of paper.

The first machine summed ten waves and was built by A. Légé & Co. for the British Association. Thomson reported that it could produce a year of tides for a port in about four hours. Machines on the same principle with more cranks followed, first for the India Office, and tide tables around the world were made this way until electronic computers took over in the 1960s.
