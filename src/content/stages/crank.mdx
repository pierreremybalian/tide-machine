This is the payoff of the first half: the machine and the water side by side, driven by the same clock. Old Brass Brains is set exactly as its operators would have set it for a new port. Each crank’s throw is the amplitude of one constituent at San Francisco, each crank’s starting angle is that constituent’s phase for the first moment of the week, and the gear trains do the rest. Nothing on the machine knows about the Moon or the bay; it only adds 37 cosine waves with a wire.

The bay on the right is built from real terrain and bathymetry. Its water is not the machine’s single number spread flat: 35 NOAA stations inside the bay each predict their own level, and the surface between them is interpolated along water paths. At Fort Point, where the pen’s station is, the two must agree, and they do.

The blue dots come from NOAA’s computers, which still use the same harmonic method today, with more constituents for some ports. Over the week the machine and NOAA differ by 0.8 cm on average (root mean square) and 2.0 cm at most; across January and July 2026 our own predictor matches NOAA to 0.8 cm RMS at San Francisco. The remaining differences come from details of the method, such as when the slow node corrections are updated, not from the machine.

Heights are above mean lower low water, the US chart datum. Relief and water are drawn three times taller than life, the light is a fixed winter afternoon, and a week of machine time passes across the scroll.
