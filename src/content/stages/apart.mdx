William Thomson, later Lord Kelvin, proposed in 1867 to the British Association that tide records be analysed into a set of simple harmonic waves whose speeds are known from astronomy. The method needs no theory of how the ocean moves. It only asks, for each speed, how big a wave of that speed is in the record and where its crests fall. Done by hand, with tables and long multiplications, a year of hourly readings took weeks. Here it takes a tenth of a second.

Each wave is fitted as two unknowns, the amounts of a cosine and a sine of its speed, which together give its height and its phase. With 31 rhythms and the mean level that is 63 unknowns, chosen together so that the sum misses the 8,760 readings by as little as possible in the least-squares sense. Rhythms with nearly equal speeds, like K1 and P1, can only be told apart because a year is long enough for them to drift through a full cycle against each other.

What is left over is not noise in the measurement. It is the water the stars did not put there: wind setting up the bay, low pressure lifting the sea, rain running out of the rivers, and the seasons, which a single year cannot pin down. That residual is why tide tables come with a warning, and it returns in stage 12, where a storm made it the biggest part of the water.
