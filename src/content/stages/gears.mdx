A tide-predicting machine keeps its astronomy in its gears. Every crank must turn at the speed of one rhythm of the tide, and those speeds are set by the Moon’s and Sun’s motions: M2 at 28.9841042° an hour, K1 at 15.0410686°, and so on. They are not simple fractions of each other or of the day.

A pair of gears turns one shaft at a fixed fraction of another, the ratio of their tooth counts, and two or three pairs in a row multiply. So the designer looks for small whole numbers whose products come close to an awkward decimal. Old Brass Brains’ designers settled for errors of up to three quarters of a degree a year on minor cranks and under a quarter of a degree on the four largest.

That was enough because no run lasted more than a year. Before each one, the operator set every crank from tables of the astronomical arguments for the first day of the year, so the drift started from zero again. Over a year, the gearing errors at San Francisco add up to less than 3 mm, much less than what wind and air pressure do to the water on an ordinary day.
