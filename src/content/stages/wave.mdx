Newton’s two bulges assume the ocean can take whatever shape the Moon asks of it, instantly. Real water cannot. A disturbance with a wavelength far longer than the depth, which the tide is, travels at the square root of gravity times depth: about 200 meters a second in the 4-kilometer-deep open ocean. To keep a bulge under the Moon at the equator the water would have to move at more than twice that. And the oceans are not a continuous shell: the Americas, Africa, Asia and Australia wall them off into basins.

So the Moon’s pull does not drag a bulge around the world. It pumps each basin, twice a day, and the water answers with a wave that runs around the basin’s edges. Earth’s rotation turns it: in the Northern Hemisphere the wave keeps the coast on its right and circles counterclockwise. In the middle of each circuit is a point where the rise on one side is balanced by the fall on the other, an amphidromic point. Lines of equal tide time (co-tidal lines) radiate from it like the spokes of a wheel, and the tide grows from almost nothing at the center to its largest at the coasts.

The map shows only M2, the largest of the tides, which repeats every 12 hours 25 minutes. The Sun’s tides and the once-a-day tides have amphidromes of their own, in different places. Stage 6 counts how many of these rhythms a coast needs.

On the North American Pacific coast, M2 runs north as a wave held against the continent. It reaches Los Angeles almost with San Diego, San Francisco two hours later and Newport, Oregon, within the next hour. Seattle is late because the wave has to come in through the Strait of Juan de Fuca and down the length of Puget Sound, in shallower water all the way.
