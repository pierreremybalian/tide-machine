The gauge near the Golden Gate is a pipe in the water with a sensor that measures the height of the sea inside it, averaged over a few minutes so waves do not count. NOAA publishes its readings every six minutes. The station has been recording since June 1854, through gold rush, earthquake and fog, and its record is one of the longest of any tide gauge in the world.

Heights here are given above mean lower low water, the zero of American nautical charts: the average of the lower of each day’s two low tides over NOAA’s reference years. A reading of 1.5 meters means the sea stands 1.5 meters above that line. The water can go below it, as it did on the morning of June 1, 2025, when the gauge read −0.07 m.

What the bay shows in a week is a mixed tide: two highs and two lows a day, of unequal height. San Francisco gets this shape from the way two families of tides, one with two cycles a day and one with one, add together. Later stages take that sum apart and show how a machine of pulleys put it back together.

The scene runs the whole week in one scroll, so each rise and fall passes in a few seconds. The water is lifted three times higher than life, along with the hills, so the drowned edges of the bay can be seen from far away.
